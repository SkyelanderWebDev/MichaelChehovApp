## 1. Fear / waiting / old habits

**Status:** internal candidate only; visually spot-checked; **not public cleared**.

**Possible surface:** common actor struggle — waiting, fear, old habits, passivity.

**Source:**
- Item: 1074
- Title/date: November 21, 1941: Michael Chekhov's fifth class for professional actors: Exercise -- Repetition is the growing power
- Source item URL: https://collections.uwindsor.ca/chekhov/item/1074
- PDF URL: https://collections.uwindsor.ca/files/original/070c5b5f7d19735af84ffa9b541595cf0a252102.pdf
- PDF page index: 10
- Verification image: `verification_images/item_1074_page_10.png`
- Attribution mode: Michael Chekhov, lesson of November 21, 1941, transcribed by Deirdre Hurst du Prey, UWindsor archive item 1074.

**Visually verified passage:**

> Answer: If we could stop all the old habits, other impulses would come which are much more subtle, much finer, more your own, more individual, more personal, because the things which you did, instead of the atmosphere, were very much "in general". Everyone can shout, everyone can go on like that, but what we are trying to do is to let the individuality come out and sparkle. It can be done only if we discard all our habits, and let our individualities respond to whatever we choose - in this instance it was the atmosphere. Then you will see that you are not poorer but actually much richer and more expressive as actors. You will see that nothing will be lost, if you will somehow switch off your old habits.
>
> Question: What you say about allowing finer, more individual things to come out of me is very appealing, but I don't understand how I can do it. In the improvisation for atmosphere the other day, I found myself involved with a lot of things. So much started to happen that I could not control, in your sense. What is the alternative to that? Must I sit back and wait for something to come?
>
> Answer: I understand your question. We must not sit back and wait - that is fear which chokes us. First of all, because you are an actor you cannot sit and wait, except if you are embarrassed, then, of course, nothing happens. For example, let us take the moment when the sailors are drinking and talking to each other in the atmosphere, there is so much to do!

## 2. Forced feelings vs action and quality

**Status:** internal candidate only; visually spot-checked; **not public cleared**.

**Possible surface:** common actor struggle — cannot find feelings / over-forcing emotion.

**Source:**
- Item: 1077
- Title/date: December 1, 1941: Michael Chekhov's eighth class for professional actors: The role of the director
- Source item URL: https://collections.uwindsor.ca/chekhov/item/1077
- PDF URL: https://collections.uwindsor.ca/files/original/9581e17a7a3a183d104d790c4db9c387d28fe2a8.pdf
- PDF page index: 4
- Verification image: `verification_images/item_1077_page_4.png`
- Attribution mode: Michael Chekhov, lesson of December 1, 1941, transcribed by Deirdre Hurst du Prey, UWindsor archive item 1077.

**Visually verified passage:**

> Secondly, the simple action, and the simple quality will appeal to our utmost inner life, as we are not touching our feelings first, we are not tearing our soul to pieces to find our feelings, which cannot be found in that way. When the actor cannot find his feelings - for instance, tender love for a child - if he tells the director that he has never had a child, the director can answer him that everyone can give the quality of tenderness and love to his actions. Simultaneously we take the action of moving the chair with the quality of "care". Neither is the result, they are only the springboard for awakening the two main things which actors must always use to the fullest extent - our will, or action, and our feelings. For action we must just choose what to do or the action, and for the future feelings, we must choose how to do it, or the quality.

## 4. Flying over the play / finding main feeling and action

**Status:** internal candidate only; visually spot-checked; **not public cleared**.

**Possible surface:** actor-struggle support and directing footnote crossover — seeing the whole rather than getting lost in parts.

**Source:**
- Item: 647
- Title/date: December 12, 1936
- Source item URL: https://collections.uwindsor.ca/chekhov/item/647

**Visually verified passage:**

> Fly over the play in retrospect - beginning from the last moment of the play and working backwards. Imagine that the curtain has come down and try to think of the whole play. From the end to the beginning, try to remember all the things we have spoken about.

## 6. Radiation / tension / wings

**Status:** internal candidate only; visually spot-checked; **not public cleared**.

**Possible surface:** daily inspiration; actor struggle — tension, weakness, body/voice dependence.

**Source:**
- Item: 839
- Title/date: February 28, 1938
- Source item URL: https://collections.uwindsor.ca/chekhov/item/839
- PDF URL: https://collections.uwindsor.ca/files/original/efb826e423e2e196494db41ab155c3144992aa48.pdf
- PDF page index: 1
- Verification image: `verification_images/item_839_page_1.png`
- Attribution mode: Michael Chekhov, lesson of February 28, 1938, transcribed by Deirdre Hurst du Prey, UWindsor archive item 839.

**Visually verified passage:**

> Radiation - this power of conquering the space - must be a spiritual thing. Never depend upon your body or your voice, because they are too weak. The spiritual motivation must be there. If we do the psychological gesture without this inner spiritual power, it is nothing: The psychological gesture is a lever for the actor's whole being. Each slight movement from radiation and inner power must be like an event. Without any tension. Psychological tension is the same as physical tension - it stops you. When you radiate, you must want to come out of your own skin. Don't be tense, but don't be weak - we often confuse these two things. Each word, each sentence must be something which has wings.

## 12. Beginning/middle/end and feeling of the whole movement

**Status:** internal candidate only; visually spot-checked; **not public cleared**.

**Possible surface:** daily inspiration; actor struggle — not anticipating the end / losing the whole.

**Source:**
- Item: 633
- Title/date: November 27, 1936
- Source item URL: https://collections.uwindsor.ca/chekhov/item/633
- PDF URL: https://collections.uwindsor.ca/files/original/8afcf96e8ebc310f3a73624f7bcc0fb15b1e59b4.pdf
- PDF page index: 1
- Verification image: `verification_images/item_633_page_1.png`
- Attribution mode: Michael Chekhov, lesson of November 27, 1936, transcribed by Deirdre Hurst du Prey, UWindsor archive item 633.

**Visually verified passage:**

> Every rhythmical movement consists of a beginning, a middle part, and an end in each case. When we have a feeling for the beginning, the middle, and the end, we will be able to feel the whole movement. When we start at the beginning, we must feel the middle and the end. When we are in the middle, we must feel the past and the future; and when we are at the end, we must feel the middle and the beginning. When you have finished and are out of this pattern, then you have the feeling of the whole which has happened. The same is true before you start. You must feel the beginning, the middle, and the end just as well as you do when you have finished and have experienced them. This is one of the principles which we must elaborate later on.
>
> When we don't anticipate the end, we are not ready to stop instantly. We start too late because we have not anticipated. We don't anticipate or remember sufficiently and so don't develop the feeling for the whole, which can only be developed by realizing that each event comprises a beginning, middle, and end.

**Verification note:** New visual check in this pass. Good readability.

## 13. The audience’s most precious moment after the curtain

**Status:** internal candidate only; visually spot-checked; **not public cleared**.

**Possible surface:** daily inspiration / directing crossover — after-effect, atmosphere, audience.

**Source:**
- Item: 663
- Title/date: February 3, 1937
- Source item URL: https://collections.uwindsor.ca/chekhov/item/663
- PDF URL: https://collections.uwindsor.ca/files/original/7746e3355a480c0b4cf1c2a7eb5c862436b9c7d9.pdf
- PDF page index: 5
- Verification image: `verification_images/item_663_page_5.png`
- Attribution mode: Michael Chekhov, lesson of February 3, 1937, transcribed by Deirdre Hurst du Prey, UWindsor archive item 663.

**Visually verified passage:**

> It is wrong for a play to end when the curtain goes down as it does in the theatre today, because if we have acted for the whole evening the result of the performance is always after the curtain is down. The better the performance, the more the audience will feel that it does not want to move. The actors must remain for a few moments in the atmosphere at the end of the play. We must establish a new habit to act after the fall of the curtain. Gradually the audience will understand that this is the most precious moment.
